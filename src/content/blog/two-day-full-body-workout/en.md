Yes, two full-body sessions a week can build muscle and make you stronger. You will usually progress more slowly than someone who does more sets or trains more often, but you can still progress. In a large analysis of trials in healthy adults, every training prescription compared did better than no training ([Currier et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579494/)).

Keeping what you already have takes much less. A review concluded that younger adults can hold on to their strength and muscle size for months on one session a week, as long as the weights stay heavy ([Spiering et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)).

## An example two-day plan

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. "3 × 6–8" means three sets of six to eight reps. Once you are past the first couple of weeks, stop most sets one to three reps short of failure. Those spare reps are called reps in reserve (RIR), and the [guide to RIR-based autoregulation](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains how to judge them.

**Day A (11 sets)**

- Back squat 3 × 6–8
- Bench press 3 × 6–10
- Romanian deadlift 2 × 8–10
- Seated cable row 3 × 8–12

**Day B (11 sets)**

- Trap-bar deadlift 2 × 6–8
- Overhead press 2 × 6–10
- Leg press 2 × 8–12
- Lat pulldown or chin-up 3 × 6–12
- Incline dumbbell press 2 × 8–12

Leave two or three days between the sessions, for example Monday and Thursday or Tuesday and Saturday. Both train your legs, so they need the gap. Back-to-back days can work if they are all you have.

No trap bar? Do a conventional barbell deadlift. No leg press? Do split squats or goblet squats for the same sets and reps.

## How the plan is built

Every session trains your legs, an upper-body push and an upper-body pull, using lifts that work several joints at once. That is the minimum a review of time-efficient training recommends ([Iversen et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)). The hinges (Romanian deadlift and trap-bar deadlift) cover the back of your legs and hips.

The plan has 22 hard sets a week. A hard set is a working set that ends fairly close to failure, so warm-ups don't count. Grouped by movement, squat and leg press get 5 sets, the two hinges 4, the three presses 7 and the two pulls 6. Each pattern reaches the same review's floor of four hard sets per muscle group per week, though the arms get only indirect work. The review suggests ten or more sets per muscle if you want more muscle, which needs extra sets or a third day.

To change the amount of work:

- **Fewer sets:** two sets of every lift, 18 a week (8 on Day A, 10 on Day B). Take the last set of each lift closer to failure.
- **More sets:** a third set on the Romanian deadlift, trap-bar deadlift, overhead press, leg press and incline press, 27 a week.

Sets that end closer to failure seem to build more muscle, while strength gains are similar across a wide range of effort ([Robinson et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/)).

## Your first sessions back

These are common coaching practice, not tested rules.

- **Start light.** For the first week or two, pick weights you could lift for about four more reps than the plan asks. Expect some soreness at first.
- **Warm up.** Before the first working set of each big lift, do two or three lighter sets with fewer reps, building up to your working weight.
- **Rest between sets.** Rest until your breathing has settled and you feel ready to match the last set. Squats and deadlifts usually need longer than rows or presses.
- **Then progress.** Once the weights leave one to three reps in reserve, follow the progression below.
- **If you miss a week,** pick up where you left off. After two weeks or more away, many coaches drop the weights a little for one session and build back from there.

## How to keep progressing

[Progressive overload](/blog/progressive-overload-guide) means gradually doing more over time. Each lift in the plan appears once a week, so you progress week by week.

Double progression is a simple way to do it. You work within a rep range, add reps first, and add weight once every set reaches the top of the range. Here is an example for bench press 3 × 6–10, with each set stopping one or two reps short of failure:

- Week 1: 60 kg for 8, 8, 7
- Week 3: 60 kg for 10, 9, 8
- Week 5: 60 kg for 10, 10, 10
- Week 6: 62.5 kg for 9, 8, 8

At the heavier weight the reps fall back, and you build them up again. A typical jump is 2.5 kg for upper-body lifts and 5 kg for squats and deadlifts.

If the reps on a lift stay the same for about three weeks, coaches usually check sleep and food first, then either hold the weight for another week or add a set. Many programs also include a planned lighter week every few weeks, and the [guide to deload weeks](/blog/deload-week) explains how to run one. Over months, expect progress to slow down.

## When you can train a third day

Two simple ways to add one:

- **Rotate the sessions.** Train A, B, A one week and B, A, B the next. Each session comes round three times in two weeks, so your weekly sets go from 22 to 33.
- **Add a third, smaller session** with lifts the plan leaves out, such as lunges, dips or curls.

For muscle growth, the total sets matter more than how many days you spread them over ([Iversen et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)), so a third day helps mainly by adding sets. Strength is different. Training more often seems to help strength, with each extra day adding less ([Pelland et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)), and the top-ranked strength prescription in the Currier analysis was heavier, multi-set training three days a week. So two days is a trade-off for strength as well as for muscle.

## Is two days a week enough for you?

As a coaching judgement, two days is enough to get started or come back after a break, and to keep what you have when time is short. It also meets the World Health Organization's guideline for adults, which asks for muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week ([Bull et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/)). That guideline is about health and sets no target for muscle growth.

It is less likely to be enough if you want as much muscle as possible. The same goes, as a rule of thumb, if you have trained for years and progress has stalled. More sets per session or a third day are the usual next steps.

### Older adults

In older adults, two sessions a week brought large strength gains in one analysis of mostly short, low-quality trials, while gains in muscle size were small ([Borde et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4656698/)). The WHO also advises older adults to do varied activity that combines balance and strength on three or more days a week, to help prevent falls ([Bull et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/)). If you have a health condition, ask a doctor or physiotherapist before you start.

### If the gym isn't your main sport

If you run, practise parkour or play a team sport, two gym days is a common way to fit lifting around it. As a coaching judgement, keep heavy leg work away from the day before your hardest sport session. [Strength training for runners](/blog/strength-training-for-runners) covers scheduling.

## What the research says about the minimum effective dose

Researchers use "minimum effective dose" in a narrow sense. One group defines it as "the least amount of training required to elicit meaningful increases in 1RM strength", where 1RM is the most you can lift once ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8435792/)).

A review of six studies in resistance-trained men found that one set per exercise, one to three times a week, raised squat and bench press 1RM. The authors called the gains "suboptimal, yet significant" and said results were less clear for the deadlift, women and highly trained athletes ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31797219/)). The 2021 project ran two six-week trials, three days a week, with 25 intermediate to advanced male powerlifters in groups of four to eight. The authors concluded that powerlifters can gain strength on about three to six heavy working sets of one to five reps per lift each week. The lifters and coaches they interviewed thought this would work for about 6 to 12 weeks.

One trial shows the difference between strength and muscle, at a higher dose than the plan above. Its 34 trained men did one, three or five sets per exercise, three times a week for eight weeks, so even the one-set group did 6 to 9 sets per muscle a week, all to failure. Strength gains were similar in all groups, but muscle grew more with more sets at several sites ([Schoenfeld et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303131/)). The Pelland analysis (67 studies, 2,058 participants, mostly young men) broadly agrees. Muscle and strength both rose with weekly sets, each extra set added less, and strength levelled off sooner.

For maintenance, the Spiering review concluded that one session a week can keep strength and muscle size in younger people for up to 32 weeks, the longest period tested, provided the weight stays as heavy relative to their maximum. Older adults may need up to two sessions and two to three sets to keep muscle size. In observational records from 14,690 adults, average age 48, doing one session a week of single sets to failure, strength rose about 30–50% in the first year and had nearly levelled off by one to two years ([Steele et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)).
