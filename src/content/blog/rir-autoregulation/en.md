Your program calls for 5 reps at 80%, but on some days that weight feels far heavier than it should. RIR-based autoregulation deals with this by setting the load from how hard the set is on the day.

## What RIR Measures

Reps In Reserve asks one question: how many more reps could you have done before failure? If you rack the bar with two clean reps left, that set was 2 RIR. If you barely finish the last rep, it was 0 RIR.

RIR ties intensity to how close you are to failure, where a percentage chart ties it to a fixed number. Closeness to failure has a large effect on how much stimulus a set gives. Two lifters can move the same weight and get different training effects, depending on how close each was to failing.

## Why Fixed Percentages Fail

Percentage-based programs treat your 1RM as constant, but it changes. Poor sleep, work stress, incomplete recovery and accumulated fatigue can shift the strength you have available from one session to the next.

On a bad day, the prescribed 80% can feel like 88%. You then grind through sets that were meant to be comfortable, or you miss reps. Both add fatigue without adding much stimulus, and after a few weeks of this, lifts tend to stall.

An RIR target avoids this. With "5 reps at 2 RIR", you use more weight on a strong day and less on a rough one, and the effort of the set stays about the same.

## Calibrating Your RIR Estimates

Estimates are inaccurate until you practise them. Most lifters think they are at 2 RIR when they have closer to 4 reps left. To get more accurate:

- Now and then, take a set to failure on a safe accessory exercise and compare the result with what you predicted.
- Watch bar speed. A clear slowdown from one rep to the next is the most reliable sign that you are running out of reps.
- Estimate RIR straight after every working set and log it. Accuracy improves with practice.
- Trust your estimates most between 0 and 3 RIR. Further from failure, estimates are much less accurate for everyone.

Most people get reasonably accurate within a few weeks of deliberate practice. Compound lifts take longer than isolation exercises.

## RPE vs RIR

RPE stands for rating of perceived exertion. In lifting it usually means a 1 to 10 scale built on reps in reserve, so the two describe the same thing from opposite ends. RPE 10 is a set with nothing left, RPE 9 leaves one rep, and RPE 8 leaves two. To convert, subtract the RPE from 10.

The scale was described and tested in [a 2016 study by Zourdos and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/) on experienced and novice squatters.

The difference is mostly in how the question is asked. RIR asks you to count: how many more reps could you have done? RPE asks you to rate how hard the set felt. Older RPE scales from endurance sport rate overall effort, not closeness to failure. If a program says RPE 8, check which scale it means. In the gym it nearly always means two reps in reserve.
