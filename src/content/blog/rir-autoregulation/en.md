RIR stands for reps in reserve: the number of good reps you could still have done when you ended a set. If you rack the bar and two more reps were there, that set was 2 RIR. If the last rep was all you had, it was 0 RIR.

Programs use RIR to say how hard a set should be without fixing the weight. "Three sets of six at 2 RIR" means you pick a weight you could lift about eight times, and stop at six. Choosing the weight from how the sets feel on the day is called autoregulation.

Your estimates don't have to be perfect. People tend to stop a rep or two earlier than they think, and they judge best when the set is hard and short.

Advice in this post without a linked study is common coaching practice, not a tested rule.

## RPE vs RIR

RPE stands for rating of perceived exertion. In lifting it usually means a scale from 1 to 10 that is built on reps in reserve, so the two describe the same thing from opposite ends. To convert, subtract the RPE from 10:

- **RPE 10** is 0 RIR, nothing left
- **RPE 9** is 1 RIR
- **RPE 8** is 2 RIR
- **RPE 7** is 3 RIR
- **RPE 6** is 4 RIR

Half points mean you aren't sure. RPE 8.5 is one rep left for certain, and maybe two.

The scale was described and tested in a [2016 study by Zourdos and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/) on experienced and novice squatters. Older RPE scales from endurance sport rate overall effort, which is a different thing. If a lifting program says RPE 8, it nearly always means two reps in reserve.

## How to use RIR in your training

### Pick a target

- **Big barbell lifts:** 1–3 RIR. A missed squat costs more than a missed curl, and technique tends to slip near failure.
- **Machines and small lifts:** 0–2 RIR. They are safer to take close to failure.
- **Deload weeks and first sessions back:** about 4 RIR. The [deload week guide](/blog/deload-week) and the guide to [two-day training](/blog/two-day-full-body-workout) both use this.

### Find the weight

Warm up, then take a guess at the first working set. Afterwards, ask how many more reps you had.

If you are on target, keep the weight. If you are off, change it for the next set. In one trial, lifters changed the load by 2% for every half point they were outside the target range, so about 4% for each rep ([Helms et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5877330/)). Changing the weight by 2–4% for each rep you are off, rounded to the plates you have, is a good starting rule.

Here is an example with a target of 3 × 6 at 2 RIR:

- **Set 1:** 100 kg for 6, and it felt like 4 RIR. That is two reps too easy, so add about 5%.
- **Set 2:** 105 kg for 6, about 2 RIR. Keep it.
- **Set 3:** 105 kg for 6, about 1 RIR. You are getting tired, which is normal. Take off 2.5–5 kg if the program wants 2 RIR on every set.

Next week, start at 105 kg and judge again.

### On a bad day

The same target takes care of it. If your usual 105 kg feels like 0 RIR on the first set, drop to about 100 kg and carry on. One lighter day needs no other change. If the same lift has gone backwards for several sessions, look at sleep, food and how long it has been since a lighter week.

### Use it to progress

RIR also tells you when to add weight. If last week's 80 kg for 8 was 2 RIR and this week's is 3 or 4, you have become stronger and the weight can go up. The [progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload-guide) covers this with a four-week example.

## How to judge RIR more accurately

- **Find out what failure is.** Now and then, take the last set of a safe exercise to the point where you can't finish a rep, and compare it with what you predicted. Machines, cables and dumbbell curls are good choices. Don't do this with a heavy squat or a bench press without safeties or a spotter.
- **Guess during the set.** Say the number to yourself with a few reps to go, then see whether it was right. Predictions made closer to failure are more accurate.
- **Watch the rep speed.** When reps slow down although you are pushing as hard as you can, you are close. In the Zourdos study, slower reps went with higher RPE ratings in both groups.
- **Keep the reps low to moderate.** Estimates get much worse in sets of more than about 12.
- **Write it down.** Log the RIR next to the weight and reps straight after the set. It is easy to remember a set as harder than it was.
- **Use the same standard.** A rep counts only if its depth and control match the others.

If you are new to this, expect to underestimate. A set that you call 2 RIR may be nearer 3 or 4. The practical fix is to aim one rep harder than feels natural on your machine and small lifts, and leave the big lifts as they are.

## RIR or percentages?

Percentage programs set the weight from your one-rep max (1RM), the most you can lift once. They are easy to follow and need no judgement. Their weak spot is that the max is a number from one day.

How much does it move? A review of 32 studies that repeated a 1RM test found a typical variation of about 4% ([Grgic et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7367986/)). Part of that is ordinary measurement error. On a day when your max is 4% lower, a weight meant to be 80% is really about 83%. You would notice it, but for most people it won't ruin a session. Percentages drift more when the tested max is months old, or when it came from a better-rested you.

RIR suits people who train alone, have irregular sleep or work, or also play a sport. Percentages suit people who tend to hold back, or to overdo it, when left to choose. Many programs combine them, with a percentage for the first guess and RIR to correct it.

## When RIR works badly

- **Your first months of lifting.** You haven't been near failure often enough to know what it feels like. Following fixed sets and reps with small increases is simpler.
- **Very high reps.** In a set of 25, burning muscles and breathlessness arrive long before failure does.
- **Jumps, throws and fast lifts.** You stop these when speed drops, so reps in reserve doesn't describe them.
- **Training through pain.** Pain changes what a rep feels like, and RIR can't tell you whether a movement is safe. Ask a physiotherapist or doctor.

## What the research does and doesn't show

### How accurate people are

A meta-analysis of 12 studies and 414 people found that they had, on average, about one more rep in them than they predicted, with large differences between studies. Predictions were better close to failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer. Training status made no clear difference ([Halperin et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)).

Single studies vary around that average:

- In 24 trained lifters on the bench press at 75% of 1RM, estimates of 1 and 3 RIR were off by about 0.65 of a rep on average ([Refalo et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37967832/)).
- In 25 trained men squatting 70% of 1RM for about 16 reps, calls of 1 RIR were off by about two reps, and calls of 5 RIR by about five ([Zourdos et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747900/)).
- In two small experiments with 14 and 24 people who had trained for at least a year, sets of knee extensions stopped about two reps early, with an uncertainty range from zero to four ([Armes et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7785525/)).

Evidence on experience is mixed. One study of 141 people saw a tendency for experienced trainees to predict better ([Steele et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5712461/)), and several others did not.

Practice may help. In 26 younger and older adults, errors shrank by up to 2.3 reps over six sessions of estimating and then going to failure ([Wiedenmann et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13499321/)). There was no group that skipped the practice. In nine trained men who did the same kind of exercise on the bench press for six weeks, accuracy did not clearly improve ([Remmert et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37436724/)).

### Whether it builds more strength

A 2022 meta-analysis compared percentage loading with autoregulated loading, using either RIR or bar speed, in trained lifters. The six studies showed similar strength gains. The average favoured autoregulation by about 2 kg, but the uncertainty included no difference ([Hickmott et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/)). Another review of eight studies and 166 people found a clearer advantage for autoregulated methods, with the largest effect for a method that doesn't use RIR ([Zhang et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7994759/)). In the Helms trial, 21 trained men gained similar strength and muscle either way over eight weeks.

So RIR seems to work at least about as well as percentages. Whether it works better is open.

### How close to failure to train

A 2024 analysis found that muscle growth increased as sets ended closer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR. The authors called it exploratory, because they had to estimate RIR from each study's description ([Robinson et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/)). The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 overview of reviews found that training all the way to failure did not consistently change results ([Currier et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). If muscle is the goal, staying within about three reps of failure on most sets is a reasonable reading of both.
