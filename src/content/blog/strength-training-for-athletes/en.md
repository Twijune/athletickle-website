Two gym sessions a week, built on a few heavy lifts and kept away from your hardest sport sessions, is a good default for most athletes. During the competition season, one session a week held on to strength for about three months in two small studies.

Endurance work is unlikely to ruin your strength gains. Across 43 studies, adding aerobic training to lifting made no meaningful difference to maximal strength or muscle growth, though gains in explosive strength were somewhat smaller ([Schumann et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/)). Leaving a gap between hard sessions may help.

Advice in this post without a linked study is common coaching practice, not a tested rule. It is written for adults who play a sport and can already lift. If you mainly run, the guide to [strength training for runners](/blog/strength-training-for-runners) goes deeper.

## What lifting does for a sport

Getting stronger in the squat tends to go along with getting faster. In a meta-analysis of 15 studies, groups that gained more squat strength also improved more in sprints, with an average sprint improvement of about 3% ([Seitz et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25059334/)). A narrative review makes the same case for jumping and changing direction ([Suchomel et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26838985/)).

Lifting doesn't replace practice. A stronger squat gives you more force to work with, and you still have to learn to use it at the speed and angles your sport needs. Keep the gym for general strength and let your sport sessions do the specific work.

## What to lift

Cover five patterns over the week:

- a squat, such as a back squat, front squat or leg press
- a hinge, such as a deadlift, trap-bar deadlift or Romanian deadlift
- a single-leg lift, such as a split squat or step-up
- an upper-body push
- an upper-body pull

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 overview of 137 systematic reviews found that strength responds best to loads of 80% of your one-rep max or more, for two or three sets, done early in the session, at least twice a week. For power it favoured moderate loads lifted fast and a low total number of reps ([Currier et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). Most people can do about five to eight reps at 80%, so sets of three to six reps that stop short of failure land in the right range.

### Two-day example

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. Stop main lifts about two reps short of failure, or two reps in reserve (RIR). The [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains how to judge that.

**Day A (13 working sets)**

- back squat 3 × 5
- bench press 3 × 5
- Romanian deadlift 2 × 8
- chin-up or lat pulldown 3 × 6–8
- standing calf raise 2 × 10

**Day B (12 working sets)**

- trap-bar deadlift 3 × 4
- split squat 2 × 8 per leg
- overhead press 3 × 6
- one-arm dumbbell row 2 × 8
- side plank 2 × 30 seconds per side

If your sport is already full of sprinting and jumping, you don't need more of it in the gym. If it isn't, two or three sets of a few full-effort jumps before the first lift is a common addition.

## How to fit it around your sport

Plan around the hardest sessions of the week: matches, hard intervals, long sparring, heavy jumping or anything that leaves your legs tired the next day. Then place the lifting.

- **Leave the day before your hardest session clear of heavy leg work.** If you have to lift that day, train the upper body.
- **Separate hard sport and lifting by six hours or more, and preferably a day.** In 58 amateur rugby players, squat and bench press gains were smaller when aerobic work followed lifting straight away than with a gap of 6 or 24 hours. Aerobic fitness improved most with 24 hours between them ([Robineau et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25546450/)).
- **If you must do both in one visit, lift first when strength is the priority.** That order gave better leg strength in a meta-analysis of ten studies and made no difference to aerobic fitness ([Eddens et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5752732/)), though a newer review is less sure. If skill or speed is the point of the day, do the sport first while you are fresh.
- **Keep easy days easy.** Some athletes lift in the evening of a hard sport day so the next day is a real rest day. That works for experienced lifters.

### Example week: team sport with a Saturday match

Example plan, not an Athletickle program.

- Monday: Day A
- Tuesday: team practice
- Wednesday: Day B
- Thursday: team practice
- Friday: rest or a light session
- Saturday: match
- Sunday: rest

The match is the hardest session in this week, and the last heavy leg work is three days before it. Each practice comes the day after lifting, which is the compromise in a week this full. If one practice is much harder than the other, cut a set or two from the leg work the day before it.

### Example week: cycling or another endurance sport

- Monday: Day A
- Tuesday: easy ride
- Wednesday: hard intervals
- Thursday: Day B
- Friday: easy ride or rest
- Saturday: long ride
- Sunday: easy ride or rest

Day A is two days before the intervals and Day B is two days before the long ride.

## Through the year

### Off season

This is the time to build. Two sessions a week is enough for most people, and three works if the sport load is low. Use [progressive overload](/blog/progressive-overload-guide): add reps first, then weight.

### Pre-season

Sport training ramps up, so hold the gym work steady. Keep the weights and trim a set from each lift when practice gets hard. Try to avoid introducing new lifts here, because the soreness will land on your sport sessions.

### In season

One session a week is a realistic minimum. In a study of 14 professional footballers, those who lifted once a week kept the strength and 40-metre sprint speed they had built in pre-season for 12 weeks. Those who lifted every second week lost some of both ([Rønnestad et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21873897/)). Six well-trained cyclists who lifted once a week held their strength and thigh muscle through 13 weeks of the competition period ([Rønnestad et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20799042/)).

Keep the weights heavy and cut the sets. For example, squat 2 × 4, bench press 2 × 5, Romanian deadlift 2 × 6 and a pull 2 × 6, early in the week.

If a full session won't fit, split it. A meta-analysis in well-trained people found no clear difference in strength between training frequencies over 6 to 12 weeks, so its authors suggest two or three very short sessions as an alternative to one or two long ones ([Cuthbert et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363540/)).

### Lighter weeks

Count all your hard training when you plan a lighter week. If the gym gets easier in the same week that your sport gets harder, you haven't had one. The [deload week guide](/blog/deload-week) has an example.

## If your sport is parkour

Parkour already loads the legs with jumps and landings, so the gym is mainly for strength. The section for parkour in [how to jump higher](/blog/how-to-jump-higher) shows one way to place two gym days around three practice evenings.

## What the research does and doesn't show

The interference effect is the idea that endurance training blunts the gains from lifting. The meta-analyses don't fully agree on it:

- An early one found smaller gains in muscle and strength when lifting was combined with running, but not with cycling ([Wilson et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)).
- Schumann and colleagues found no meaningful cost to maximal strength or muscle size, and no difference between running and cycling. Explosive strength gains were smaller, mainly when both were done in one session.
- Petré and colleagues looked only at leg strength, in 27 studies. Trained people gained less when they combined the two. Untrained people did not, and the result for moderately trained people was unclear. Among the trained, the effect appeared only when both were done in the same session ([Petré et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170/)).
- A 2024 review of 59 studies found a small cost to leg strength in men and none in women. The women's estimate was less certain, and the authors asked for more studies in women and in highly trained athletes ([Huiberts et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)).
- A 2026 umbrella review of 17 meta-analyses found comparable strength, power and muscle growth with and without endurance work, and no clear effect of session order. Its authors noted that data from highly trained athletes remain scarce ([Held et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41762427/)).

Read together, they suggest that any cost is small, that explosive strength and already-trained legs are the likeliest to pay it, and that spacing the sessions may reduce it.

Some limits apply to the advice above. Seitz reported a correlation across studies, so it can't show that a stronger squat caused each sprint gain. The two in-season studies had only six or seven people in each group. Robineau tested aerobic work after lifting, so it says less about lifting after practice. The concurrent-training reviews mostly measure steady or interval cardio, which is only part of what team sports and combat sports involve. We could not find a trial that compares weekly layouts like the two above.

Soreness works in the other direction too. A review notes that lifting sessions that cause muscle damage can impair endurance performance for a while afterwards ([Doma et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30847824/)), which is another reason to keep hard leg work away from key sessions.
