Progressive overload means doing a little more over time, so that your body keeps having a reason to adapt. "More" can be weight, reps or sets. For most people the simplest way to do it is to work in a rep range, add reps until every set reaches the top, then add weight and start again at the bottom.

You don't have to add something every session. Some weeks you will repeat last week's numbers, and that is a normal part of the plan.

Advice in this post without a linked study is common coaching practice, not a tested rule. It assumes you already know the lifts.

## The plan

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. Three full-body days a week, for example Monday, Wednesday and Friday. "3 × 6–8" means three sets of six to eight reps. Stop most sets one or two reps short of failure, which is called 1–2 reps in reserve (RIR). The [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains how to judge that.

**Day 1 (12 sets)**

- Back squat 3 × 6–8
- Bench press 3 × 6–10
- Barbell row 3 × 8–10
- Lateral raise 3 × 12–20

**Day 2 (11 sets)**

- Romanian deadlift 3 × 8–10
- Overhead press 3 × 6–10
- Lat pulldown 3 × 8–12
- Biceps curl 2 × 10–15

**Day 3 (12 sets)**

- Leg press 3 × 8–12
- Incline dumbbell press 3 × 8–12
- Seated cable row 3 × 8–12
- Leg curl 3 × 10–15

Keep the exercises the same for all four weeks. If you change them every week, you have nothing to compare.

## How to progress each week

Follow the same four steps for every lift:

1. **Write down** the weight and the reps of each set.
2. **Next time, try to beat the total reps** at the same weight, even by one.
3. **When every set reaches the top of the range,** add weight next time. Use the smallest jump you have: often 2.5 kg for upper-body barbell lifts, 5 kg for squats and deadlifts, and one step up on dumbbells and machines.
4. **The reps will drop back** towards the bottom of the range. Build them up again.

This is called double progression. The extra rep only counts if it looks like the others. A squat that gets higher every week, or a row that uses more swing, is not progress even though the log says so.

### A four-week log for the squat

Here is a lifter doing 3 × 6–8:

- **Week 1:** 100 kg for 6, 6, 6 (18 reps)
- **Week 2:** 100 kg for 7, 7, 6 (20 reps)
- **Week 3:** 100 kg for 7, 6, 6 (19 reps), after two bad nights of sleep
- **Week 4:** 100 kg for 8, 8, 7 (23 reps)

There was no new weight in four weeks, and one week went backwards. It is still clear progress, because the same weight went from 18 reps to 23. In week 5 the lifter gets 8, 8, 8, and in week 6 moves to 105 kg for 6, 6, 6.

### Small lifts need wide ranges

Going from 10 kg to 12 kg dumbbells on a lateral raise is a 20% jump. That is why the plan gives it 12–20 reps. You might take several weeks to get from 12 to 20 with the 10s, then drop back to about 12 with the 12s.

## Four ways to add

- **Weight.** The obvious one. It is the best choice if your goal is a bigger one-rep max, because practice with heavy weights is specific to that.
- **Reps.** The easiest to use week to week, since one rep is a smaller step than the smallest plate.
- **Sets.** Useful when reps and weight have stalled for a few weeks and you recover well. Add one set to one or two lifts and leave the rest alone.
- **Better reps.** Full range, controlled lowering, less swing. It doesn't show in the log, but the other three only count if the reps stay the same.

Change one at a time. If you add weight and sets together and it goes badly, you won't know which was too much.

## When progress stops

One bad session tells you very little. If a lift has not improved for about three weeks, work through this list:

1. **Is the log honest?** Check that depth, pauses and help from a spotter haven't changed.
2. **Are you eating, sleeping and resting enough between sets?** Two or three minutes between hard sets of big lifts is a reasonable minimum.
3. **Are the sets hard enough?** Sets that stop five or six reps short may not be doing much for muscle growth.
4. **Is everything stalling, or one lift?** If everything is, fatigue is a likely cause, and a lighter week makes sense. The [deload week guide](/blog/deload-week) shows how to run one. If it is one lift, try adding a set, changing the rep range, or swapping it for a close relative.
5. **Are you simply further along?** Progress slows down for everybody. Judge an experienced lifter's progress over months.

## After the four weeks

Take a lighter week if you feel you need one, then run the same plan again from your new numbers. Two or three rounds before changing exercises is common. If you only have two days, the [two-day full-body workout](/blog/two-day-full-body-workout) progresses in the same way. If you want to vary sets, reps and loads over several months, read about [periodization models](/blog/periodization-models).

If you are coming back after weeks off, start lighter than your old log says and build up before you try to beat it.

## What the research does and doesn't show

### Progressing or standing still

Two recent trials compared the same training with and without progression, both in untrained people. In 55 young women who trained one arm movement for eight weeks, muscle thickness grew about 23% when the weight went up whenever they reached the top of 8–12 reps, and about 12% when weight and reps stayed fixed ([Kassiano et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41718594/)). In 31 young adults who progressed one leg and not the other for 12 weeks, muscle size was not clearly different at any single test, but the progressed leg grew more over the last three weeks. By then, sets on the other leg ended about four reps short of failure ([Ferrari et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42880344/)).

Standing still worked for a while, then. A fixed weight gets easier as you get stronger, and progression keeps the sets hard.

### Weight or reps

We found two trials in which people progressed only one way. In 43 people with at least a year of leg training, one group added weight in the same rep range and the other added reps at the same weight, for eight weeks. Muscle growth and strength were similar, with a slight lean towards reps for one thigh muscle and towards weight for strength. The authors considered the strength difference of questionable practical value ([Plotkin et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528903/)). In 39 untrained young people, whose two legs were trained in the two different ways for ten weeks, strength and muscle size rose about equally ([Chaves et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38286426/)).

Both trials were short. Nobody can keep adding reps at one weight for years, so in practice you use both.

### How much to add

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 guidance was to add 2–10% once you can do one or two more reps than planned ([ACSM, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). Its 2026 update draws on 137 systematic reviews and still recommends progressive training. It found that few of the program details it examined made a consistent difference. Heavier loads helped strength, and ten or more weekly sets per muscle helped growth ([Currier et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)).

### Adding sets

More weekly sets go with more growth and strength, with each extra set adding less, and strength levels off sooner ([Pelland et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)). A trial in 31 trained men looked at adding sets during a program. Over 12 weeks, groups that raised their weekly sets every two weeks gained more squat strength than a group that kept them constant. The difference in muscle size was not clear ([Enes et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796222/)). That doesn't mean sets can rise forever. The trial ended after 12 weeks.

### How long progress lasts

In training records from 14,690 adults doing one short session a week, strength rose about 30–50% in the first year and was close to a plateau after one to two years ([Steele et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)). That is a minimal dose, so more training might go further. In competition results from 1,897 powerlifters, the strongest men improved about half as fast as the least strong men ([Latella et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7448836/)).

We could not find a trial that compares double progression with other ways of scheduling increases. It is popular because it is simple to follow.
