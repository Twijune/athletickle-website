Jumping higher is trainable. Make your legs stronger with squats and similar lifts, and practise full-effort jumps (plyometrics) two or three times a week. Each works on its own, and combining them works whichever way you arrange it ([Brandt et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37459857/)).

Expect a gain of a few centimetres over two to three months of consistent training. In controlled trials, plyometric programs added roughly 2–6 cm (about ¾ to 2½ inches) on average over about two months, using roughly 70–85 jumps per session, two to three sessions a week ([Markovic, 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2465309/)). We could not find research supporting the gains of 25 cm (10 inches) or more promised in some videos and programs.

A few centimetres can be enough if you are just short of a target, such as a rim or the top of a wall. If you are 20 cm short, one program is unlikely to get you there.

## Start here if you don't lift yet

This is common coaching practice if you jump a lot but have never lifted. Learn the basic lifts first, from a coach or a gym induction.

- **Squat.** Start with a goblet squat, holding a dumbbell or kettlebell against your chest, or a squat with an empty 20 kg barbell. Go as deep as you can while your heels stay down and your back keeps its shape. Deep squats carry over to jumping better than quarter squats ([Hartmann et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22344055/)).
- **Pick the weight.** Choose a weight you could lift about three more times at the end of each set: three reps in reserve (see the [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation)). When every set feels easier, add a little weight.
- **Romanian deadlift.** With a bar or dumbbells in front of your thighs and knees slightly bent, push your hips back until the backs of your thighs stretch, then stand up.
- **Where to jump.** Jump on a firm, non-slip surface: a gym floor, track or grass. For drop jumps, start from a box, step or low wall 20–30 cm high.
- **Measure your jump.** Stand side-on to a wall, chalk your fingertips and mark your standing reach. Jump and touch as high as you can. The gap between the marks is your jump. Use the same shoes and warm-up, and take the best of three.

## What jump height depends on

Jump height comes from how hard you push into the ground in the short time your feet are on it, relative to your body weight. So the main levers are strength relative to body mass ([Nuzzo et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18438251/)) and how quickly you build force ([McLellan et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20093963/)), and extra mass that adds no force lowers your jump. Technique matters less than many guides suggest ([Vanezis and Lees, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16338725/)), with one clear exception.

## How to train for a higher jump

### Swing your arms

In one lab study of 20 men, jumping with an arm swing added about 8.6 cm compared with keeping the arms at the sides ([Lees et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15519601/)). Swing both arms back as you dip and drive them up hard as you leave the ground.

### Get stronger

Squats, Romanian deadlifts and split squats are the base. Stop most sets one to three reps short of failure and add weight gradually ([progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload-guide)). Olympic lifts such as cleans showed no clear difference from plyometrics ([Hackett et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26626268/)) but need in-person coaching.

### Add plyometrics

Plyometrics are full-effort jumps and hops, usually with a quick dip or a landing straight into the next push. Examples are the squat jump (from a held squat, no dip), the countermovement jump (the ordinary jump: dip quickly, then go), the broad jump, single-leg hops and the drop jump (step off a low box and jump as soon as you land). Mix several types and don't add weight to the jumps ([de Villarreal et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19197203/)).

As common coaching practice:

- Make every jump a full effort, and rest a minute or two between sets.
- Land with hips and knees bending together and knees in line with your toes.
- End the set when the jumps get clearly lower or the landings get heavy and loud.
- Start well below the trial volumes and build up while the jumps stay high and the landings quiet, as in the example week.

### Combine them

Lifting and jumping in the same session, alternating, or on separate days all worked, and none clearly won. Contrast sets are a convenient way to fit both into one session: a heavy set followed by a few jumps with a similar movement, such as squats then countermovement jumps. Any extra height on the day is small.

## Example week

Example week, not an Athletickle program. Two sessions at least two days apart. Jump counts leave out warm-up jumps.

**Week 1, if you are new to structured jump training**

- Monday: squat 3 × 5 at about three reps in reserve. After each set, rest two to three minutes, do 3 countermovement jumps, then rest two minutes before the next set (9 jumps). Squat jumps 3 × 5 (15). Romanian deadlift 3 × 8. Total 24 jumps.
- Thursday: countermovement jumps 3 × 5 (15). Broad jumps 3 × 5 (15). Split squat 3 × 8 each leg. Total 30 jumps.

Week total: 54 jumps.

**Week 8, what it builds to**

- Monday: squat 3 × 5, heavier but still at two to three reps in reserve, with the same jumps after each set (9). Squat jumps 4 × 5 (20). Broad jumps 4 × 5 (20). Drop jumps from 20–30 cm 4 × 5 (20). Romanian deadlift 3 × 8. Total 69 jumps.
- Thursday: countermovement jumps 4 × 5 (20). Broad jumps 4 × 5 (20). Single-leg hops 4 × 4 each leg (32). Split squat 3 × 8 each leg. Total 72 jumps.

Week total: 141 jumps, each session at the low end of the trial range. In between, add about one set (5–10 jumps) per session each week, bringing in drop jumps and hops around week 4. If you already train jumps, start nearer the middle.

## For parkour practitioners

A parkour evening of precisions, drops and runs can hold dozens of landings, so count them. As a coaching judgement, if you practise three times a week you may already be near the trial volumes, and the gym is mainly where you add strength. Keep the gym jumps near the week 1 level and progress the lifts instead.

One way to fit two gym days around three parkour evenings:

- Monday: gym
- Tuesday: parkour
- Thursday: parkour, lighter on big jumps
- Friday: gym, strength first and the jumps cut short
- Saturday: parkour, your biggest jumping day
- Wednesday and Sunday: rest or easy movement

Cut the gym jumps first in a week with a long jumping session, and in a [deload week](/blog/deload-week), ease the parkour jumping too.

## How long it takes

Most trials lasted two months or less, so longer-term data are scarce. As common coaching practice, give a program eight weeks before you judge it and retest every four weeks.

## Jump volume and your knees

Jumping and landing load the tendons. Jumper's knee (pain in the patellar tendon below the kneecap) is common in jump-heavy sports. In one study of elite Norwegian athletes, 14.2% had it, including 44.6% of volleyball players ([Lian et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15722279/)). In young elite volleyball players, more training hours and more match play were linked with higher odds of developing it ([Visnes and Bahr, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22260424/)).

As general advice, add jump volume gradually and count all your jumping, parkour, sport and gym, as one total. See a physiotherapist or doctor if tendon pain lasts, gets worse during or after sessions, or changes how you move.

## What the research does and doesn't show

**How much, and at what dose.** The 2–6 cm is Markovic's rough conversion of pooled gains of about 5–10% across 26 controlled trials. Participants were 83% male, mostly young adults with some children, and mostly recreationally active. Markovic noted signs of publication bias for two jump tests, so the true effect may be somewhat smaller. He found drop-jump, countermovement-jump and mixed programs gave similar gains. De Villarreal's larger meta-analysis of 56 studies disagreed: mixing types worked better, and the largest gains came in programs of more than 10 weeks and 20 sessions with more than 50 jumps per session.

**Strength.** In a 10-week trial of 59 men and women, deep squats raised jump height and quarter squats did not. Hackett's studies were in athletes and PE students with lifting experience, and only three compared Olympic lifts with plyometrics. The findings on what jump height depends on come from small studies of men.

**Combining and contrast.** Brandt's analysis of 24 studies found no arrangement better than another, with or without a strength-training background. Whether combining lifts and jumps in one session adds anything long term is uncertain. Two analyses found a small extra gain in countermovement jump height over lifting alone ([Thapa et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39163393/), [Zhao et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12903741/)). Zhao's covered trained people only, with three direct studies. Compared with jump-type (ballistic) training alone, one found extra gains in squat jump height and strength but not in countermovement jump ([Thapa et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41731268/)). Certainty was low or very low throughout. The short-term boost after a heavy set was small for jumps ([Seitz and Haff, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26508319/)) and, for power, peaked after 7–10 minutes of rest ([Wilson et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22580978/)). Most of these studies used short warm-ups, so it is unclear how much it adds after a thorough one ([Blazevich and Babault, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6838751/)).

**Who and how long.** A scoping review of 242 plyometric studies found most lasted seven weeks or less, few included women, and nearly half mixed in other training ([Ramirez-Campillo et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470823/)). In healthy adults, tendons became stiffer with training, mainly when the load was heavy. Five plyometric programs gave mixed results, and that analysis did not look at pain or injury ([Bohm et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4532714/)). We could not find any routine shown to prevent tendon problems.
