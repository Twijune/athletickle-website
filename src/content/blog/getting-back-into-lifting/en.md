Start with fewer sets and lighter weights than you want to, and build back over about four weeks. You have probably lost some strength and size, but regaining it is usually much quicker than building it was the first time.

In one trial, people who had trained for ten weeks and then stopped for ten were back at their pre-break level after about five weeks of training ([Halonen et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/), [university summary](https://jyu.fi/en/news/breaks-in-resistance-training-do-not-impair-long-term-development-in-strength-and-muscle-size)). They were new to lifting before the study, so treat five weeks as encouraging and don't plan around it as a deadline.

This post is for people who stopped because of work, travel, family or lost interest. If you stopped because of an injury, an illness, surgery or a pregnancy, ask the doctor or physiotherapist who treated you what you can start with.

Advice in this post without a linked study is common coaching practice, not a tested rule.

## How light to start

Your muscles and tendons have lost some of their tolerance for hard training, and you will remember your old numbers better than your body does. These are starting guesses for a lift you used to do regularly:

- **Up to two weeks off:** carry on roughly where you stopped. Take a little weight off for the first session if it feels heavy.
- **Three to eight weeks off:** use about 10–20% less weight and drop a set from each lift in the first week.
- **Two months to a year off:** use about 20–30% less weight and do two sets per lift.
- **More than a year off:** ignore the old numbers. Start with a weight that feels easy and add to it from session to session.

Whatever the guess says, check it against the set. In the first week, finish every set feeling you could have done at least four more good reps. Those spare reps are called reps in reserve (RIR), and the [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains how to judge them. If a set feels harder than that, take weight off.

## A four-week example plan

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. It assumes about three months off and two full-body sessions a week, for example Monday and Thursday. Week 1 uses two sets of each lift.

**Day A**

- Back squat, 8 reps
- Bench press, 8 reps
- Seated cable row, 10 reps
- Romanian deadlift, 8 reps

**Day B**

- Leg press or split squat, 10 reps
- Overhead press, 8 reps
- Lat pulldown, 10 reps
- Trap-bar or conventional deadlift, 6 reps

The plan moves one thing at a time:

- **Week 1:** 2 sets of each lift, at least 4 RIR
- **Week 2:** 2 sets, about 4 RIR
- **Week 3:** 3 sets, about 3 RIR
- **Week 4:** 3 sets, about 2 RIR

That takes you from 16 working sets in week 1 to 24 in week 4. Warm up for the first big lift of the day with two or three lighter sets.

### What that looks like on one lift

Say you used to squat 90 kg for three sets of eight, with two reps in reserve.

- Week 1: 70 kg for 2 × 8, with five or six reps to spare
- Week 2: 75 kg for 2 × 8, about 4 RIR
- Week 3: 80 kg for 3 × 8, about 3 RIR
- Week 4: 85 kg for 3 × 8, about 2 RIR

Week 1 is meant to feel too easy. The limit that week is how sore you will be two days later. If week 4 feels like a struggle, repeat week 3.

After week 4, go back to a normal plan. The [two-day full-body workout](/blog/two-day-full-body-workout) picks up at about this level, and the [progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload-guide) covers how to keep adding.

### If you trained three or four days before

Come back at two or three days for the first two weeks, then add a day. If you want to return to your old split straight away, halve the sets. You can get back to your old number of days in a few weeks, and there is little to gain by doing it in the first one.

## Soreness in the first week

Expect some. Hard training that your muscles aren't used to makes them sore for a few days, and the lowering part of each rep causes most of it. Past training gives some protection, and that protection fades. In a small study of hard lowering exercise for the elbow flexors, students were less sore the second time when it came six months after the first. After twelve months there was no protective effect left ([Nosaka et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11528337/)).

Mild soreness is no reason to skip the next session. Use the same weights or go slightly lighter, and move on when it has eased. If a muscle hurts too much to use normally, wait.

See a doctor the same day if your urine turns dark, or if the pain, swelling or weakness is far worse than normal soreness. Either can be a sign of severe muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis), which can follow a first session that was far too hard.

## Mistakes that slow the return

- **Testing your max in week 1.** It tells you little that the sets won't, and it is a hard session on unprepared muscles.
- **Doing your old program at lower weight.** The number of sets causes much of the soreness, so cut both.
- **Adding everything back at once.** If you are also going back to running or a sport, stagger the starts by a week or two.
- **Rushing because it feels easy.** Strength often returns in jumps. Let two good sessions in a row decide when to add weight.

## If you are an older lifter

Breaks cost older lifters more. In a meta-analysis of 103 studies, the loss from stopping was larger in people over 65 ([Bosquet et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)). The return still works. In 30 older men who trained for 12 weeks and stopped for 12, strength and power fell by 5–15%, and their one-rep max was back in less than eight weeks of retraining ([Blocquiaux et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32017951/)). Give yourself closer to eight weeks than four, and check with a doctor first if you have a health condition.

## What you keep if you can't stop completely

If the break hasn't started yet, or another one is coming, one session a week goes a long way. A review concluded that younger people can keep strength and muscle size for up to 32 weeks on one session a week and one set per exercise, provided the weights stay as heavy relative to their maximum ([Spiering et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)). Older adults may need two sessions and two or three sets to keep muscle size.

A planned lighter week is a different thing from a break. The [deload week guide](/blog/deload-week) covers that.

## What the research does and doesn't show

**Short breaks.** In 20 young trained men, leg press strength did not drop after two weeks off, and they kept their lean mass ([Hwang et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)). The Bosquet meta-analysis found that longer breaks cost more, and that inactive people lose more maximal force and power than recreational athletes.

**Repeated breaks.** Fourteen young men bench pressed for 24 weeks. Half trained straight through and half took two three-week breaks. Muscle size and strength ended up similar ([Ogasawara et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)). In 60 women the authors describe as detrained, a two-week break halfway through ten weeks of training made no clear difference to strength gains ([Gentil et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26595206/)).

**A ten-week break.** Halonen and colleagues randomized 55 untrained adults, of whom 42 finished. One group trained for 20 weeks in a row and the other took ten weeks off in the middle. Strength, muscle size and jump height all fell during the break. Both groups finished level. The break group regained leg strength and muscle faster in their first five weeks back than the other group was gaining at the same stage of its training. The university's summary adds that strength held up better than muscle size, and that the break group needed 30 weeks to reach what the others reached in 20. So the break cost time.

**Why it comes back quickly.** "Muscle memory" is real as an observation, and the cause is not settled. Muscle fibres seem to keep some of the extra nuclei they gain from training. One study of 12 people confirmed that, but it found no clearly better response to retraining, and its authors said the benefit "remains to be determined" ([Cumming et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39159314/)). Another found that some muscle proteins stay raised after ten weeks without training ([Hulmi et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40183698/)).

We could not find a trial comparing different ways of returning after a break, and most retraining studies started with untrained people. That leaves the percentages and the four weeks above as coaching practice. If you trained for years before stopping, you have more to regain than those participants did, and the studies can't tell you how long it will take.
