Periodization training means planning in advance how your sets, reps and loads change over weeks and months. Linear periodization moves from more reps at lighter loads to fewer reps at heavier loads over several months. Weekly undulating periodization changes the rep range each week, daily undulating changes it each session, and block periodization runs short, focused phases one after another.

If you have stalled and wonder whether a different model would fix it, the answer is probably not on its own, because the differences between models are small. Check your volume, effort and recovery first. If you have only ever run linear plans, trying an undulating plan for a couple of mesocycles is reasonable. That is coaching judgement, informed by the research summarized at the end.

Compared with non-periodized training, planned variation helps strength a little ([Moesgaard et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)). For muscle growth, the pooled studies show no model ahead.

## What periodization is

A periodized plan sets out how volume (the amount of work, such as sets and reps), intensity (the load on the bar) and focus change over time ([Evans, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6351492/)). Whatever the model, you still need to do a little more over time, as the [progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload-guide) explains.

## Macrocycles, mesocycles and microcycles

A periodized plan nests three time scales:

- **Microcycle:** the shortest repeating unit, often a week.
- **Mesocycle:** several microcycles, often about a month.
- **Macrocycle:** the whole plan, often a year.

Lengths vary by source. One clinical review uses the same words for longer units, with 3–4 month mesocycles and 1–4 week microcycles, so check what a program means by each term. As common coaching practice, gym programs often use four-week mesocycles, with a lighter [deload week](/blog/deload-week) at the end.

## The main models, with example weeks

Example weeks, not an Athletickle program. The three-day examples use Monday, Wednesday and Friday and three main lifts: squat, bench press and barbell row. "3 × 8" means three sets of eight reps. Every working set stops about 2 reps short of failure, or 2 reps in reserve (see the [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation)), so fewer reps always means more weight.

Two rules apply to the linear and undulating examples, both common coaching practice:

- **Progression:** when the last set of a lift leaves you more than about 2 reps in reserve, add the smallest load step you have the next time you do that lift at that rep range.
- **Deload:** every fourth week is a lighter deload week.

### Linear periodization

The rep range stays put for several weeks, then drops while the load rises.

**Example week, weeks 1–3:** on all three days, squat, bench press and barbell row at 3 × 8 (9 sets a session, 27 a week).

Weeks 5–7 move to 3 × 6, heavier, and weeks 9–11 to 3 × 4, heavier again. Weeks 4, 8 and 12 are deloads.

### Weekly undulating periodization

Every session in a week uses the same rep range, and the range changes the next week.

**Example weeks, one mesocycle:**

- **Week 1:** squat, bench press and barbell row at 3 × 10 on all three days
- **Week 2:** the same lifts at 3 × 5, heavier
- **Week 3:** the same lifts at 3 × 8, at a load between the other two
- **Week 4:** deload

The next mesocycle repeats the wave with slightly heavier loads.

### Daily undulating periodization

Daily undulating periodization (DUP) changes the rep range from one session to the next.

**Example week, three days:**

- **Monday:** squat, bench press and barbell row at 3 × 8
- **Wednesday:** the same lifts at 3 × 6, heavier
- **Friday:** the same lifts at 3 × 4, heavier again

That is 27 sets a week, the same as the linear example. Only the timing differs.

**Example week, four days:**

- **Monday, lower body:** squat, Romanian deadlift and split squat at 3 × 8
- **Tuesday, upper body:** bench press, barbell row and overhead press at 3 × 8
- **Thursday, lower body:** the same lower-body lifts at 3 × 5, heavier
- **Friday, upper body:** the same upper-body lifts at 3 × 5, heavier

That is 9 sets a session and 36 a week, with each lift done once with more reps and once heavier. Split squat reps are per leg.

### Block periodization

Block periodization runs short phases in sequence, each concentrating on a small number of qualities ([Issurin, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20199119/)). A common order is accumulation (more volume at moderate loads), transmutation (heavier, more specific work) and realization (low volume, heavy or sport-specific work), with phases of about 2–4 weeks ([Lorenz and Morrison, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4637911/)).

**Example week, accumulation phase (weeks 1–3):** on all three days, squat, bench press and barbell row at 4 × 10, then split squat and dumbbell shoulder press at 3 × 12 (18 sets a session).

In the transmutation phase (weeks 4–6), the main lifts might move to 4 × 5, heavier, plus one accessory at 3 × 10 (15 sets a session). In the realization phase (weeks 7–8), only the main lifts might remain, at 3 × 3 and heavier again (9 sets a session).

Real programs often mix models, so yours may already change rep ranges within the week while getting heavier across months.

## Which model suits whom

This section is coaching judgement, informed by the research below.

**Stalled after a year or more of lifting.** Before changing model, check four things. Are you doing enough hard sets? Are your sets close enough to failure? Are you sleeping and eating enough to recover? Are you taking a lighter week now and then? If those are in order and you have only run linear plans, try weekly or daily undulating. Some evidence favours undulating plans for trained lifters, and some does not.

**"DUP doubles your gains."** That claim usually traces to [one 2002 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11991778/) of 20 men, and the difference in absolute bench press gains was not statistically significant. Pooled studies find a modest advantage for undulating plans at best, and not all of them find one.

**New to lifting.** In untrained people, studies find little or no difference between models. A simple plan that you will follow, with steady load increases, is the usual coaching choice.

**Muscle growth.** The pooled studies show no model ahead. One meta-analysis advised focusing "on training volume and progressive overload" ([Grgic et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571788/)).

Whatever you choose, give it one or two mesocycles (about four to eight weeks) before judging it.

### If you also play a sport or do parkour

When the gym supports another sport, that sport's calendar and load come first. A sensible approach is to place your hardest gym phases in weeks when practice is lighter, and to count jumping, sprinting and landing as training load too. We could not find a study comparing periodization models in parkour practitioners.

## What the research does and doesn't show

### Periodized versus non-periodized training

Two meta-analyses found periodized training raised one-rep max (1RM) strength more than non-periodized training. One pooled 18 studies and called the effect moderate ([Williams et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)). [The other](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/), limited to studies with equal total volume, found a small 1RM advantage with a wide confidence interval, and no difference in muscle growth.

A 2019 review checked the Williams meta-analysis and an older one from 2004. In all 21 of their studies, the comparison group followed a constant program, with the same sets and reps throughout ([Afonso et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6692867/)). Those studies support varying your training. They did not test whether a planned sequence beats unplanned variation.

### Comparing the models

A meta-analysis of 17 studies found no strength difference between linear and undulating plans ([Harries et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/)). The [volume-matched meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/) found a small 1RM advantage for undulating plans overall. In trained participants the effect was moderate (0.61), but only just statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval running from no effect to a large one. Untrained participants showed none. A 2026 meta-analysis of 29 studies found the two models comparable ([Zhang et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12999919/)). For muscle growth, a [meta-analysis of 13 studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571788/), 12 of them in untrained people, found linear and daily undulating plans "likely to be similar".

The 2002 trial behind "DUP doubles your gains" had 20 young men with at least two years of lifting, all on linear-style programs. In percentage terms the DUP group gained about twice as much (28.8% against 14.4% on the bench press), but it started much weaker on that lift, which inflates percentages. The difference in absolute bench press gains was not statistically significant. The between-group difference was statistically significant over the first six weeks but not over the last six, although the DUP group still gained more on average. The authors suggested the change of program, rather than DUP itself, might explain the result.

Block periodization in lifting rests on few studies, too few for the volume-matched meta-analysis to pool. In 31 college track and field athletes, block and DUP training showed no significant strength difference ([Painter et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22173008/)). In 24 trained men, block training may have raised upper-body power more than a traditional periodized plan ([Bartolomei et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24476775/)). In 17 women, the same lab found bigger lower-body strength gains with weekly undulating training than with block training, and only the undulating group's thigh muscle growth was statistically significant ([Bartolomei et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25807030/)).

### Limits and open questions

A review of 42 randomized periodization trials found none longer than nine months ([Afonso et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28344448/)). In the 17-study meta-analysis, trials averaged about 12 weeks and participants about 24 years, although periodization is meant to work over months and years.

We could not find a study testing the common claim that periodization prevents injuries or plateaus. The theory itself is debated: one researcher argues that it rests on outdated stress research ([Kiely, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856877/)), and others defend it ([Cunanan et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29307100/)).
