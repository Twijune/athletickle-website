Yes, strength training is worth doing as a runner. Twice a week, with heavy lifts, jumps or both, it tends to make distance runners a little more economical, meaning they use less energy at a given pace. A meta-analysis of heavy and explosive programs put the average gain at about 4%, which its authors called small ([Denadai et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27497600/)). Race results improved in most trials that tested them ([Blagrove et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889786/)).

It won't make you noticeably heavier. In most of those trials, body weight did not change while strength rose. Over a few months, expect your legs to gain a little muscle and the scales to move very little.

It needn't wreck your long run either. Lifting rarely changed aerobic fitness in runner trials. A hard session can leave your legs tired the next day, so the trick is spacing, covered below.

Lifting is also often sold as injury prevention. For runners, that evidence is much weaker than for sport in general.

## If you have never lifted

This is common coaching practice for the first few weeks.

**Your first session.** Pick four or five basic lifts: a goblet squat (holding one dumbbell at your chest), a dumbbell Romanian deadlift, a split squat, a calf raise and a dumbbell row. Learn them first from a coach or a gym induction, then do two sets of 8 to 10 reps each.

**Your starting weight.** You don't need to know your one-rep max, the most you could lift once. Choose a weight you could lift about four more times when you finish the set. That margin is called reps in reserve (RIR), and the [guide to RIR](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains how to judge it. If you're unsure, go lighter and treat the first set as a test.

**Soreness.** Expect stiff legs for a day or two after the first sessions. It usually fades within a week or two. An easy run while sore is fine.

**Building up.** Each week, add a set or a little weight when the reps feel easy. After three or four weeks, move towards the heavier sessions below. The [progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload-guide) shows how.

## What to do in the gym

### Which lifts

Most coaches cover four patterns:

- a squat or leg press
- a hinge, meaning a bend at the hips with a flat back, as in a deadlift or Romanian deadlift
- a single-leg lift, such as a split squat, step-up or lunge
- a calf raise

Keep the calves in. One small trial linked gains in calf strength to better economy ([Eihara et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39523854/)). Upper-body and trunk work are reasonable extras, kept short.

### How heavy

Heavier loads have the best support for economy ([Llanos-Lagos et al., 2024a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/)). Once you know the lifts:

- main lifts: 2–4 sets of 3–6 reps, stopping 1–2 reps short of failure
- single-leg and calf work: 2–3 sets of 6–10 reps, stopping 2–3 reps short

Avoid going to failure, which adds fatigue and may slow recovery.

### Jumps

Plyometrics are quick jumps and hops with short ground contacts. Adding them to lifting may help further ([Llanos-Lagos et al., 2024b](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11258194/)). Good choices are skipping, low hops in place, bounding and small drop jumps from a 20–30 cm box. Start with 30 to 60 landings a session. Trials built mainly on jumps worked up to about 110–230 over six to nine weeks. Common coaching practice is to do them first, while you are fresh.

### Two-day example

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. Use it once the beginner weeks feel routine.

**Day A** (13 working sets)

- back squat 3 × 5, about 2 RIR
- Romanian deadlift 3 × 6, about 2 RIR
- split squat 2 × 8 per leg, about 2 RIR
- standing calf raise 3 × 8
- one-arm dumbbell row 2 × 8

**Day B** (11 working sets, plus jumps)

- optional jumps first: low hops in place and skipping, about 40 landings in all
- trap-bar deadlift 3 × 4, about 2 RIR
- step-up 3 × 6 per leg
- seated calf raise 3 × 10
- dumbbell bench press 2 × 8

Many coaches add a lighter [deload week](/blog/deload-week) every few weeks. The [two-day full-body workout guide](/blog/two-day-full-body-workout) covers this format in more detail.

## How to fit lifting around your running

A hard run means intervals, a tempo run, a race or your long run. An easy run, at a pace where you could chat, doesn't count. The 2018 review recommends:

- at least 24 hours between a lifting session and your next hard run
- at least three hours after a hard run before you lift

If you must run and lift in one session, lift first if strength gains are the priority. The order made no difference to aerobic fitness ([Eddens et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5752732/)).

### Example week

Example plan, not an Athletickle program. It suits three or four runs a week with a Sunday long run:

- Monday: Day A
- Tuesday: easy run
- Wednesday: intervals or tempo run, or another easy run
- Thursday: Day B
- Friday: easy run, optional
- Saturday: rest
- Sunday: long run

Each lift leaves more than 24 hours before the next hard run, and nothing heavy comes the day before your long run. Experienced lifters often lift on the evening of a hard run so easy days stay easy, but that is a lot for a beginner.

### Racing season and race week

The 2018 review suggests dropping to one session a week in the racing season, and that suggestion rests mainly on one small study. In another study, gains were lost within about six weeks once lifting stopped, so keep that one session going. We could not find a trial on race week. Common coaching practice is a short session early in the week with fewer sets, then no lifting in the last few days before a key race.

## For other athletes

As coaching judgement, cyclists, triathletes and team-sport players can use the same pattern: two sessions a week with heavier loads, kept away from the hardest sport sessions, and often one in the competition season. A widely cited narrative review argues that stronger athletes tend to jump, sprint and change direction better ([Suchomel et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26838985/)).

## What the research does and doesn't show

### Economy, race times and weight

The 2018 review covered 24 trials that added heavy lifts, explosive lifts, jumps or a mix to running. Economy improved in 14 of the 20 that measured it, at least at some speeds, and time trials improved in 8 of 12. Most trials lasted 6 to 14 weeks, and 352 of the 469 runners were men. Body weight did not change in 18 trials.

A 2024 meta-analysis found that heavy loads improved economy. Loads of 40–79% of one-rep max showed no clear effect, but only three studies tested them. We could not find a direct comparison of heavy and moderate loads. A companion paper found a moderate effect of heavy loads on time trials and time to exhaustion, rated moderate certainty. Programs that added jumps to lifting showed a larger effect, but that came from six small studies and was rated very low certainty.

Not every trial found a gain. In 19 well-trained female endurance athletes who ran and cycled, 11 weeks of heavy strength training raised leg strength by about 40%, yet neither economy nor a 40-minute all-out run improved compared with usual endurance training ([Vikmoen et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4783109/)). The lifters' average weight rose from 62.4 to 63.1 kg while the non-lifters lost a little. In 20 middle-aged recreational runners, both groups (lifting or jumps) used about 2% less oxygen after 10 weeks, but neither ran 5 km faster, and there was no running-only group.

The in-season advice rests on a 40-week study of 20 runners, 11 of whom lifted, that was not randomized. In season, its one weekly session was jumps and explosive lifts.

### Does lifting slow your running?

The interference effect is the idea that endurance training blunts gains from lifting. An early meta-analysis found that endurance work blunted power gains, and running, but not cycling, was linked to smaller gains in muscle and strength ([Wilson et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)). A 2022 update of 43 studies found no meaningful cost to maximal strength or muscle size, but smaller explosive strength gains, mostly when both were done in the same session ([Schumann et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/)). The advice to lift first comes from 10 studies, mostly in untrained people. Trained people saw smaller leg-strength gains only in same-session training ([Petré et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33751469/)).

Those studies measured strength, not running. In the runner trials, adding lifting rarely changed VO2max, your maximal aerobic capacity.

### Injuries

In other sports, mostly team sports, a meta-analysis of six trials with 7,738 people found strength training cut injuries to about a third ([Lauersen et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30131332/)). A later review noted that half of those trials used adolescent athletes.

That review pooled nine trials and 1,904 runners. Exercise programs did not clearly reduce injury risk or rate, and the authors rated that result moderate to high certainty, although most single trials were of low quality ([Wu et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127851/)). Only four programs included strength exercises, mostly bodyweight and low in volume. A post hoc analysis of the three small supervised trials found fewer injuries, possibly because people did the exercises. In 720 first-time marathon runners, a self-directed 10-minute routine three times a week found no clear difference in overuse injuries that stopped people finishing, 7.1% against 7.3% ([Toresdahl et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31642726/)).

We could not find a trial of heavy gym lifting with running injuries as the outcome, so for runners an injury benefit is possible but unproven. If pain changes how you run or keeps getting worse, see a physiotherapist or doctor.
