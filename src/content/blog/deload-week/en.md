A deload week is a week of easier training. You keep your usual training days and main lifts, but you do fewer sets, use a little less weight and stop further from failure. The aim is to let the fatigue from several hard weeks come down before the next block.

Coaches typically prescribe five to seven days and schedule one every four to six weeks, although the spacing varies a lot ([Bell et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2022.1073223)).

Most deload advice comes from coaching experience, and the trials so far are few and small. They suggest a lighter week costs you little. The bigger benefits often promised for it have not been shown.

## How to run a deload week

The rules below are common coaching practice, not a tested prescription.

### Cut your sets

Volume is the amount of work you do, usually counted as hard sets per muscle or per session. Coaches in one interview study described cutting it by anything from about a quarter to more than half, and halving the sets is a common choice ([Bell et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2022.1073223)). In a survey of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, about four in five cut their weekly sets and about half also cut the reps per set ([Rogerson et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y)).

### Lighten the load so you stop further from failure

"Intensity" can mean two things. Load is the weight on the bar. Effort is how close to failure you go, often counted in reps in reserve (RIR): the extra reps you could still have done. The [guide to RIR-based autoregulation](/blog/rir-autoregulation) explains it in more detail.

More than four in five survey athletes lowered the load on their main lifts during a deload, and a similar share stopped further from failure. In practice, pick a weight that leaves about four reps in the tank for the reps you are doing. That usually means a modest drop. There is no need to strip the bar right down. A review of maintenance training concluded that exercise intensity, meaning relative load, "seems to be the key variable for maintaining physical performance" ([Spiering et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)). It found too little data on athletes to give them specific advice.

### Keep your days and main lifts

Most survey athletes kept their usual training days and exercises. Squats, presses, rows and deadlifts stay in. Small accessories such as curls are the easiest thing to drop. Keep sessions the same length or shorter.

### A sample week

This is an illustration, not a prescription from any study and not what either Athletickle app prescribes. It is a four-day upper and lower body split, where "4 × 6" means four sets of six reps and normal sets stop at about 1 to 2 RIR.

In the deload week, the days, main lifts and reps stay the same. The sets roughly halve, the curls and pushdowns go, and the weight comes down enough that each set stops about four reps short of failure.

**Monday, lower body**

- Normal: squat 4 × 6, Romanian deadlift 3 × 8, leg press 3 × 10, calf raise 3 × 12 (13 sets)
- Deload: two sets of each lift, one set of calf raises (7 sets)

**Tuesday, upper body**

- Normal: bench press 4 × 6, barbell row 4 × 8, overhead press 3 × 8, lat pulldown 3 × 10, biceps curl 3 × 12 (17 sets)
- Deload: two sets of each lift, no curls (8 sets)

**Thursday, lower body**

- Normal: deadlift 4 × 5, split squat 3 × 8 per leg, leg curl 3 × 10, calf raise 3 × 12 (13 sets)
- Deload: two sets of each lift, one set of calf raises (7 sets)

**Friday, upper body**

- Normal: incline bench press 4 × 8, pull-up 4 × 6–8, dumbbell shoulder press 3 × 10, cable row 3 × 10, triceps pushdown 3 × 12 (17 sets)
- Deload: two sets of each lift, no pushdowns (8 sets)

That takes the week from 60 working sets to 30.

### During the week

Use the lighter sets to practise technique. Walks and easy cycling are fine. Avoid these:

- testing your max or taking sets to failure
- filling the spare time with new hard training, such as a long run
- making up the missing sets the following week
- taking the whole week off by default (in the trial described below, a week off probably cost a little strength)

## Do you need one now?

### Planned or reactive

A planned deload is written into the program in advance. A reactive deload is taken when you notice you need one, which coaches call autoregulation. In the survey, about half the athletes planned their deloads, and most of the rest combined a plan with reacting to how they felt. We could not find a trial comparing the two. A plan means the deload happens even in weeks when you feel fine and would skip it, while reacting fits your actual response better, as long as you read the signs honestly.

### Signs lifters go by

The most common reason survey athletes gave was simply that the program said so. Next came feeling beaten up (soreness, joint aches or pain) and stalled performance. The usual signs look like this:

- The same weights feel heavier, or reps drop at loads you handled recently.
- Soreness is still there when the next session comes round.
- Joints or tendons ache more than they usually do.
- You feel worn down, physically and mentally.

What about a lift that has felt heavy for three sessions running? One heavy session means little, because sleep, stress and food all move it. Three in a row on the same lift is starting to look like a pattern. If other lifts are slipping too, or you dread going to the gym, many coaches would take the lighter week now, or bring a planned one forward. If only one lift is stuck and everything else feels normal, the problem may be that lift's programming rather than overall fatigue.

Pain is a separate question from fatigue. As a general rule, see a physiotherapist or doctor if pain in a joint or tendon changes how you move, gets worse during or after sessions, or is still there after the lighter week.

### How often

The [common habit among coaches](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2022.1073223) is a lighter week every four to six weeks, and the survey athletes averaged about every five to six. A 2023 consensus study of strength coaches set no fixed number and suggested adjusting the spacing to how you respond ([Bell et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0)). We could not find a trial that compares one deload frequency with another. The authors of the trial described below suggested that studies of young lifters lasting nine weeks or less, at up to 90 sets a week, may not need a deload for recovery.

### Where it fits, and the week after

If your program already has a lighter week built in, that is your deload. If it doesn't, the usual approach is to add one between blocks, so the program's own weeks stay in order. The week after, go back to the program where you left off, with your normal sets and the loads you were using before.

## If lifting isn't your main sport

If you also do parkour, run or play a sport, count all of your hard training, not only the gym. Jumping and landing load your legs and tendons as well. A deload that halves your gym sets but keeps every hard jump session leaves much of that load in place. As a general coaching judgement, ease the overall load that week, with fewer hard jumps and drops and more easy movement alongside the lighter gym sessions.

## What the research does and doesn't show

The researchers behind that 2023 consensus study proposed this definition, based on their coach panel's answers and earlier studies: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training". All of the coaches in the final round agreed that scientific studies on deloading are limited, and that current practice rests mainly on coaching experience.

[Coleman et al. (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/) is the only randomized trial we found that compared a deload (a full week off) with training straight through. Its 39 finishers had at least a year of lifting experience and trained four days a week for nine weeks. Muscle thickness, jump height and muscular endurance in the legs ended up similar in both groups. Lower-body strength gains were probably a little smaller in the group that took week five off, though the uncertainty was wide. There was no sign that the break led to more muscle growth overall. Anecdotally, participants often said they felt "out of practice" afterwards. The authors suggested that a lighter week, rather than full rest, might avoid this. A commercial training company funded the study and, according to the paper, had a role in its design and in preparing the manuscript.

Two other studies looked at lighter weeks. In 30 trained men, a very light week after six weeks of high-volume training produced largely similar responses to a week off ([Vann et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34138821/)). Most of its outcomes were molecular, and the abstract does not say how the men were allocated to groups. In 19 untrained young men, two reduced-volume weeks within eight did not appear to hold back gains in muscle thickness or 10-rep maximum in leg extensions and biceps curls ([Pancar et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5)).

Longer breaks do cost strength and power, and a meta-analysis of 103 studies found the loss grows with the length of the break ([Bosquet et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)). In untrained adults, though, strength and muscle lost over ten weeks off came back quickly on retraining ([Halonen et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)).

Many coaches believe deloads reduce injuries, and the consensus panel agreed they "could be used" for that. The authors called for more research on injury. We found no study showing that deloads boost muscle growth, or that any particular frequency is best.
